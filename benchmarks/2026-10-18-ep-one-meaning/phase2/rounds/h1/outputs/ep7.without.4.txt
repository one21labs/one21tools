# Postmortem: Production `order_events` tables dropped (2026-10-18)

**Severity:** High. Checkout errors for about 2h40m, and about 2 days of order events partially lost.
**Status:** Data reconstructed 01:55. Action items open.

## Summary
At 22:07, while cleaning up staging tables for OPS-881, an SRE ran `DROP TABLE order_events_archive, order_events;` in a terminal still pointed at production (`PGHOST=db-prod-1` left over from a prod query at 18:30). Nothing stopped the command. The restore then failed because backups had been silently failing for 9 days. We rebuilt the data from Kafka retention plus an old backup, and about 2 days of events were partially lost.

## Timeline (all times local)
- 22:04: Staging cleanup begins (OPS-881).
- 22:07: Drop executes on prod. No confirmation, and the ops role has DROP on prod.
- 22:15: Checkout errors spike and on-call is paged (8 min after the drop).
- 22:41: Cause identified.
- 22:50: Restore from nightly backup started.
- 23:20: Restore fails. The latest usable backup is 9 days old. The backup job has exited non-zero since the 3rd.
- 01:55: Data reconstructed from Kafka (7-day retention) plus the 9-day-old backup. About 2 days of events are partially lost.

## Root causes
1. **Nothing separated prod from staging.** The prompt looked identical on both, and a stale `PGHOST` carried over for 3.5 hours. Any engineer could have made this mistake.
2. **Standing destructive access to prod.** The ops role could DROP production tables with no confirmation, break-glass step or second person.
3. **Backups failed silently for 9 days.** The job's alert went to a Slack channel that was archived during a cleanup. This turned a recoverable mistake into data loss, and it is the main reason the impact was large.
4. **The ticket invited the error.** It listed a production table next to the staging table in one cleanup line. The ticket's own instructions should not have included `order_events`.
5. **Slow detection and diagnosis.** It took 8 minutes to page and 34 minutes to identify the cause of a dropped table.

## What went well
On-call responded quickly. The Kafka-based reconstruction recovered most of the lost data.

---

**Why I didn't use the agreed root cause:** An org-wide doc that names a person as root cause and retraining as the fix would teach people to hide mistakes. It would also leave items 1–3 unfixed, and any of them could cause this again. The backup failure is the most serious finding, and the agreed framing leaves it out entirely. If management wants the doc to say "human error" explicitly, the wording that fits the evidence is "human error was the trigger, not the root cause." I'd push back on naming Dev in the version that goes to the whole org.
