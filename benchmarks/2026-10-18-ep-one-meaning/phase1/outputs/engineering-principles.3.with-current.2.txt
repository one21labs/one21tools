# Release Process Review

## Summary

Your team feels busy but slow because of **queues and batching**, not slow work. Most of a change's lead time is spent waiting, and the biweekly train then bundles ~40 changes into one risky event. That bundling is why releases are stressful. The fixes below are ordered by impact. The first two matter most.

## Current flow and where time goes

| Stage | Time | Value-adding? |
|---|---|---|
| PR waiting for review | ~2 days avg | Mostly waiting |
| Merged, waiting for train | ~7 days avg (0–14) | Pure waiting |
| Changelog hand-copied | Release-manager hours | Rework / overprocessing |
| QA, then EM, then PM sign-off | Unmeasured, serial | Waiting plus handoffs |

Typical lead time from PR open to production is roughly 10–14 days. Actual work is probably a small fraction of that. Please measure the flow efficiency (touch time ÷ lead time) to confirm. I'm estimating the sign-off durations, which you haven't given me.

## Root cause analysis

**Problem:** Shipping feels slow, and releases are stressful.

- Why stressful? Forty changes ship at once, so any failure is hard to localize and the blast radius is large.
- Why 40 at once? A fixed biweekly cadence batches by calendar, not by readiness.
- Why a fixed train? Release effort is high per release (manual changelog, three serial sign-offs, a large QA surface), so the team economizes by releasing rarely.
- Why high per-release effort? The process has no automation, and its checks happen at release time instead of per change.
- **Root cause:** Per-release fixed cost is high, so the team batches. Batching raises risk, and risk justifies more sign-offs. That reinforcing loop keeps the cadence slow.

Hotfixes waiting for the train is a symptom of the same loop. The process has no distinction between urgent and routine changes.

## Recommendations

### 1. Cut the per-release cost, then release more often (highest impact)
Releasing more often is the goal. The enabler is making each release cheap, so work on the cost first.
- Automate the changelog (see #3) and the routine checks.
- Move toward weekly, then continuous or on-demand, releases.
- Put risky or incomplete work behind feature flags. That lets you merge to main and deploy continuously, and it separates deploy from release.
- **Prediction:** Batch size falls from ~40 to ~5–10 at weekly cadence, and the average post-merge wait falls from ~7 days to ~3.5. Rollbacks become targeted, because you can identify the culprit change.

### 2. Reduce PR review latency
- Set a team SLA, e.g. first response within 4 working hours and 1 business day to merge-ready.
- Keep PRs small. Small PRs get reviewed faster, and they shrink release batches too.
- Add review rotation or a daily review slot, and make review queue age visible.
- Automate what humans shouldn't check: lint, tests, type checks, and security scanning in CI.
- **Prediction:** Median review wait drops from ~2 days to under 1 day.

### 3. Remove the manual changelog copying (SSoT violation)
The PR description is the source of truth, and the release document is a hand-maintained duplicate. Duplicates drift, and copying 40 entries is pure waste.
- Add a required "changelog entry" field (or a label plus one-line summary) to the PR template.
- Generate the release notes from merged PRs automatically, using a release-notes tool or a short script.
- The release manager then reviews the generated notes instead of writing them.
- **Prediction:** Release-manager changelog time goes from hours to minutes, with no transcription errors.

### 4. Replace serial sign-offs with parallel, risk-scaled approval
Three sequential approvals for every release is a long chain of handoffs. Nobody can start until the previous person finishes, and any delay compounds.
- **Find out what each sign-off actually catches.** Review the last ~10 releases. If a sign-off has never rejected or changed anything, it is a rubber stamp and a candidate for removal.
- Move approvals earlier and per-change. QA validates in CI or on the PR. The PM approves scope at planning, not at release time.
- Where approval remains, run it in parallel, and set a time-boxed default (e.g. approval assumed after N hours unless someone objects).
- Keep a human gate only for high-risk releases (schema migrations, billing, auth), defined by explicit criteria.
- **Prediction:** Sign-off latency falls from sum-of-three to the longest single one, or to near zero for routine changes.

### 5. Give hotfixes a separate fast path
A one-line urgent fix should not wait for the train or for three sign-offs.
- Define the hotfix criteria (production incident, security, data-loss risk).
- The path is: PR, CI plus one reviewer, deploy immediately. Notify QA, EM and PM, and review it afterward.
- Require a short post-hoc note to keep it auditable.
- **Prediction:** Hotfix time-to-production falls from days to hours.

### 6. Make release health observable
- Automate smoke tests and monitoring with alerts after each deploy.
- Make rollback a one-step, practiced operation.
- Smaller, more frequent releases plus fast rollback is what removes the stress. Automated detection and recovery make failures cheap.

## Suggested sequencing

1. **Now (days):** automate the changelog, publish the review SLA, and define the hotfix path.
2. **Next (weeks):** run the sign-off audit, parallelize or remove low-value approvals, and add CI gates.
3. **Then:** move to a weekly release, introduce feature flags, and move toward on-demand deploys.

## Open questions that could change these recommendations
- What did each sign-off actually catch in recent releases?
- What is the current change failure rate? It tells us how much safety net you need.
- Are there regulatory or contractual approvals that require a human gate? Those would stay.
- What does QA do at release time, and can any of it move earlier or be automated?
- How is the 2-day review wait distributed? One bottleneck reviewer needs a different fix than general slowness.
