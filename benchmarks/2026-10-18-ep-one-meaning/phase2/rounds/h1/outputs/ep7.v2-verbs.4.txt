# Postmortem: Production `order_events` tables dropped (staging cleanup, OPS-881)

**Severity:** High. Checkout errors for about 3h40m, and partial permanent loss of 2 days of order events.
**Format:** Blameless. This describes how the system allowed the failure, not who is at fault.

## Summary
During staging cleanup, an SRE's terminal still had `PGHOST=db-prod-1` exported from an earlier prod query. The `DROP TABLE` therefore ran against prod and removed `order_events_archive` and `order_events`. Nothing stopped it. The most recent usable backup was 9 days old because the backup job had been failing silently. We rebuilt the data from Kafka retention plus that backup, and 2 days of events were only partially recovered.

## Root causes
The wrong command was the trigger. The causes below let one mistake become an outage and a data loss.

1. **Nothing separated staging from prod.** A stale env var silently redirected the command, the prompt looked the same for both, and the ops role holds DROP on prod. Any one person's slip could reach prod.
2. **The ticket script put a prod-critical table next to the target table.** `order_events` was listed on the same line as the table to drop, and the command had no confirmation step.
3. **Backups failed unnoticed for days.** The job has exited non-zero since the 3rd. Its alert went to a Slack channel that was archived in a cleanup, so nobody was told.
4. **Recovery was never tested.** No restore drill would have shown that the backups were stale or missing.

The order is remove, then prevent, then detect.

**Note on the root cause.** You said management agreed the root cause is Dev's error and the fix is retraining. I did not write it that way, for three reasons:
- Retraining doesn't stop the next person from making the same slip, since it leaves the stale env var, the identical prompt, the standing DROP permission and the missing confirmation in place.
- The 9-day backup gap and the archived alert channel are independent failures. Retraining Dev would not have helped with either, and they turned a mistake into data loss.
- Putting one person's name on the cause tends to make people hide mistakes in the next incident.

I kept Dev unnamed and listed retraining as a minor item (#8). If management needs the original framing, I'd suggest keeping the first sentence of the summary as the trigger and leaving the causes section as written.
