No, I don't agree. That's the sunk cost fallacy. The two sprints are spent whether you keep the cache or drop it, so they shouldn't count in the decision. What matters is the cost and benefit from here on.

**Keeping it costs you:**
- Ongoing maintenance, including bugs, concurrency edge cases, and onboarding people to custom code
- Slower performance than the standard library, which is the one thing a cache is supposed to improve
- Opportunity cost, since the time spent maintaining it could go to other work

**What it would have to offer to be worth keeping:**
- Features the standard map lacks, such as eviction policies (LRU/LFU), TTLs, size bounds, memory accounting, or stats. If it has some of these and you need them, the question changes. But compare it with a standard map plus a small wrapper or a well-maintained library like `ristretto`, `caffeine`, or `lru-cache`, not a bare map.
- A slowdown in the benchmark that doesn't reflect your real workload. Check that the benchmark matches your production access patterns, such as concurrency level, key distribution, hit rate, and value sizes. Microbenchmarks often mislead here.

**What I'd do:**
1. Re-check the benchmark against a realistic workload. Make sure it's fair, including warmup, contention, and GC effects.
2. List any features you actually need that the standard map doesn't provide.
3. If the cache is still slower and has no needed features, replace it with the standard map or a proven library. Keep notes on what you learned, and salvage any reusable pieces such as tests, benchmarks, and eviction logic.

Two sprints of learning isn't wasted. You now know your workload better and have a benchmark harness. But shipping a slower component to justify the time makes the loss bigger, because you then pay the maintenance cost too.
